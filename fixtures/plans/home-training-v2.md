# Home Training Plan

## What this is

A four-session home plan built around an exercise mat, eight adjustable dumbbell handles,
and nothing else. Four sessions a week at roughly 45 minutes door to door, including
warming up and any weight changes.

This is version 2. Version 1 has now had a full four-week block behind it, and this
revision is what came back out of that block rather than a second set of guesses.

## What changed in version 2, and why

Nothing here touches the injury exclusions, the equipment, or how progression works —
those held up over the block and there is no reason to revisit them yet. What changed is
what a block of real sessions is supposed to change: two names, one movement out, one
movement in, and a handful of targets that week one showed were set too conservatively.

**Two ids got tidied up, not trained differently.** `goblet-squat` is now `gobletsquat`
and `rear-delt-reverse-fly` is now `prone-reverse-fly` — the second one also picks up a
shorter display name, Prone reverse fly, since "(prone)" was redundant with the note
already saying so. Neither is a prescription change; if either shows up as a fresh
exercise with no history in the app, that is this renaming, not a new movement.

**Hammer curl is gone from session D.** It sat next to dumbbell curl doing the same job
on the same load for the same reps, and session D is supposed to be the short one — the
one you drop first when the week is tight. Cutting the redundant pair gets it back
closer to that brief without losing any arm work; dumbbell curl alone still covers it.

**Session C gains a second unilateral hip-extension movement.** Single-leg glute bridge
sits next to split squats now, so that session is training single-leg hip extension the
same way it already trains single-leg knee-dominant work, rather than only in the
bilateral glute bridge everyone gets in warm-up. Session C's split squat also moves up
from the light pair to a single goblet dumbbell held at the chest — week one showed the
light pair barely working, and a single heavier load is easier to hold steady in a
staggered stance than a pair swinging at your sides.

**A few targets moved because week one left more in reserve than intended.** Session A's
gobletsquat range moves to 10–14 reps and the floor press picks up an optional fourth
set; session D's prone row rest widens to match how long you were actually resting in
session A anyway. None of this is new work, just the existing work catching up to what
week one actually showed.

**Two metrics changed.** A start-of-session sleep-quality check-in is new — squash and
lifting both read differently on a bad night, and it is cheap to ask. The per-exercise
technique rating is gone; nobody filled it in during week one, and reps-in-reserve was
already telling the same story with less friction.

## Who this is for, and what it is for

You play squash about once a week and lift on and off rather than continuously. Both
confirmed directly.

The goals below are yours, stated directly. All four were named, so the ordering is
mine — it reflects how much each one actually changes the plan, not how much you care
about each:

1. **Build and define muscle.** This is the primary driver. It is why the plan carries
   direct arm, shoulder and abdominal work rather than being purely compound movements.
2. **Get stronger.** Served by the same work, with progression that actually adds load
   over time rather than just adding reps forever.
3. **Develop the abdominals and core.** Every session ends with trunk work, and it is
   real prescribed work, not a stretch-and-go afterthought.
4. **General fitness and energy.** Squash supplies most of the conditioning. The lifting
   does not need to duplicate it.

Six months from now, this is working if pressing and rowing loads have gone up, the
sessions still happen in weeks that go badly, and the hips are no worse than they are now.

## The equipment, precisely

Eight adjustable handles, and 48 kg of plates: eight 0.5 kg, eight 1 kg, eight 1.25 kg,
eight 2 kg, four 2.5 kg. One exercise mat. No bench, no chair, no sofa edge, no step.
Plenty of floor space and a normal ceiling.

The handles are **spinlock** — threaded bar, cast-iron plates, screw collars — confirmed
from a photograph. **Each handle weighs approximately 1 kg assembled, collars included.**
That detail matters and is easy to get wrong: spinlock collars weigh 0.25–0.5 kg apiece
and there are two per handle, so if the 1 kg had been the bare bar, every load in this
plan would have been up to 1 kg per dumbbell light. It isn't. The numbers below are the
real assembled weights.

Being spinlock rather than selectorized also means a weight change costs a minute or two
of unscrewing and re-stacking, not a dial twist. That is the constraint the load plan
below is built around.

**Eight handles is the single most useful fact about this setup.** It means every load in
this plan can be built once and left assembled. Nothing in any session requires changing a
plate. That is how a 45-minute session that includes setup actually fits into 45 minutes.

Here is a plate allocation that builds all five configurations simultaneously and leaves
change to spare. Loads on each handle must be symmetrical — the same plates on both sides.

| Handles | Configuration | Plates per handle | Weight per dumbbell |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Goblet single | 4 × 2 kg, 2 × 0.5 kg | 10 kg |
| 2 | Pressing pair | 2 × 2.5 kg | 6 kg |
| 2 | Rowing pair | 2 × 2 kg (one each side) | 5 kg |
| 2 | Light pair | 2 × 1 kg, 2 × 0.5 kg | 4 kg |
| 1 | Pullover single | 4 × 1.25 kg | 6 kg |

That uses 38 kg of plates. The 10 kg left over — four 1.25 kg, four 1 kg, two 0.5 kg — is
your progression budget for this block.

**Your smallest usable increment is 1 kg per dumbbell.** A 0.5 kg plate has to go on both
sides to stay symmetrical. On a pair, that is a 2 kg jump in total load — from 12 kg to
14 kg on the pressing pair, which is a 17% increase in one step. That single fact shapes
the entire progression model below.

**Handle capacity is the one thing still unknown.** Spinlock sleeves are short, and the
threaded section is what limits how many plates fit — not the plate set. Nothing in this
plan exceeds 10 kg on any one handle, or three plates a side, so it is safe either way.
Measure it when convenient: load one handle as full as it will go with the collars still
tightening properly, and record the total. That number is the ceiling on the pressing pair
and the goblet single, and it decides when we need a different strategy rather than more
plates.

## Working around the hips and lower back

You describe symptoms around the outer sides of the buttocks, extending up toward hip
level and the lower back, worse after sleep. They are reproduced by standing side bending,
standing forward bending, picking something up from in front of you, and sitting in a
figure-4 position and then bending forward.

**Nothing here is a diagnosis.** I am not a clinician, this is not an assessment, and it
is not a rehabilitation programme — it is a training plan written to work around a
described pattern rather than to treat it. Get it properly looked at if you can, not
because the plan is unsafe without it, but because a plan written around a known problem
is better than one written around an unknown one. If you do, bring this document; the
exclusions below are the part worth discussing.

**What is deliberately not in this plan, and why:**

- **No deadlifts, Romanian deadlifts or any loaded hip hinge.** Standing forward bending
  is one of your named provocateurs. A loaded hinge is that movement with weight on it.
  This is the biggest omission in the plan and it costs you some posterior-chain work —
  glute bridges and split squats cover part of the gap, not all of it.
- **No weighted side bends and no loaded spinal rotation.** Same reason, on the lateral
  and rotational axes. Squash already supplies more trunk rotation than you need.
- **No bent-over rows, standing or supported.** This is the easiest thing to get wrong
  for this setup. Without a bench, a supported one-arm row means hinging over your own
  thigh and holding that position for the length of a set. That is a sustained version
  of a movement that hurts you. Your rowing happens face-down on the mat instead.
- **No figure-4 or pigeon stretching.** You have told me that position plus a forward
  bend reproduces symptoms. Stretching into it is not a good idea.
- **No morning sessions recommended.** Symptoms are worse after sleep. You train after
  work and on weekend afternoons anyway, which is the right end of the day for this.

**One thing that is not an exercise but matters more than most of them:** picking something
up from in front of you is on your provocation list, and every dumbbell in this plan starts
on the floor. Squat down to your dumbbells — hips back and down, chest up — rather than
bending to them. Lay everything out before you start so you are not doing that pickup
twenty times a session. This is the single highest-frequency exposure you have to a
movement that hurts, and it happens outside the workout too.

### Symptom-response rule

This is the rule that will be in front of you in the app while you train.

**Green — carry on.** Mild, familiar, stable sensation that does not build set to set and
does not change how you move. Keep going and keep an eye on it.

**Yellow — modify.** The sensation becomes clearly more noticeable, changes your
technique, or stays elevated after you finish an exercise. Cut the load, cut the range,
cut the reps, slow it down, or swap the movement. Do not push through to finish a
prescribed number.

**Red — stop.** Sharp pain, pain that keeps escalating, pain that feels different from
your familiar symptoms, anything radiating down a leg, numbness, pins and needles, or new
weakness. Stop that exercise. Do not test it again to see if it is still there. If it
persists, worsens, or changes character, get it assessed.

Testing the painful movement repeatedly to check on it is itself a provocation. Resist it.

## Why the plan looks like this

### Full-body sessions, not a split

Every session trains most of you. That is a direct response to the thing that has actually
ended plans for you before: life getting in the way. On a body-part split, a missed session
means a body part gets nothing that week and the plan starts to feel broken. Here, any
single session done in isolation is a productive week of training. Miss two and the third
still works.

The same logic drives the sequencing. GAIN suggests the next session in order but does not
enforce a calendar. Nothing in this plan needs a particular weekday. If you train Tuesday
and then not until Sunday, you do the next session in the sequence and nothing is wrong.

### Pressing and pulling without a bench

The floor press is the pressing movement because it needs no bench and self-limits the
bottom of the range — your upper arms hit the mat before your shoulders reach an
uncomfortable position. The standing overhead press covers the shoulders; the risk there
is leaning back and arching the lower back to get the weight up, which is exactly what
you should not do. Brace, squeeze the glutes, and if you catch yourself leaning, take the
seated floor version instead — sitting on the mat with legs out removes the option of
arching backwards.

Back work is the compromise in this plan. Face-down on the mat, chest supported by the
floor, rowing two dumbbells to your ribs: your spine does nothing, there is no hinge to
hold, and no support surface is required. The cost is range of motion — the floor blocks
the bottom of the movement, so you get roughly two thirds of a normal row. That is a real
loss and I would rather tell you than pretend otherwise. The floor pullover fills part of
the gap, working the lats through a range the prone row cannot reach, from flat on your
back with knees bent. Ribs down on that one — the arch it wants to produce is the one
you are trying to avoid. It is marked conditional for that reason, and its condition is
to omit rather than swap: if the lower back lifts off the mat and shortening the range
does not fix it, drop the movement. Nothing else here reaches quite the same range, so
there is no honest substitute to offer in its place.

Prone reverse flies handle the rear delts and upper back from the same face-down position,
for the same reason.

### Legs without a hinge

Goblet squats are the loaded squat pattern. Holding the weight at the chest keeps the
torso upright, which suits you — it is a squat that resists turning into a forward bend.
Split squats and reverse lunges cover the unilateral work and build hip control that has
some carryover to squash. The reverse lunge stays at bodyweight — alternating reps add up
faster than they look. Session C's split squat now carries a single goblet dumbbell
rather than the light pair it opened on: the light pair turned out to be barely a load
once the movement settled, and one dumbbell held at the chest is easier to keep steady in
a staggered stance than a pair swinging at your sides.

Glute bridges are the hip-extension work, lying on your back with the lumbar spine
supported by the floor throughout — the same job a hinge does, without the hinge. Session
C now also carries a single-leg version of it in main work, so that session trains hip
extension unilaterally the same way it already trains the squat pattern unilaterally: one
leg planted, driving through the heel, hips held level rather than letting the free side
dip.

Reverse lunges here are **alternating** — left, right, left, right within one set. That is
faster than doing a full set per side and it keeps the session moving. It matters for the
log: the rep count is the total across both legs, so eight each side is sixteen reps.
Split squats are the opposite — a full set on one leg, then the other — so those log as
two separate entries.

### Core

Dead bugs, bird dogs, side planks and front planks are the stability half: bracing,
resisting extension, resisting rotation, lateral endurance. McGill curl-ups and reverse
crunches are the direct abdominal half, because visible abdominal development is one of
your stated goals and stability work alone will not produce it.

Dead bugs, bird dogs and McGill curl-ups all alternate sides within a set, so they log as
one entry with the total rep count. Side planks are held to a side at a time, so those log
separately per side.

Reverse crunches are marked conditional. The posterior pelvic tilt they involve can
provoke lower-back symptoms in some people. If it reproduces your familiar sensation,
swap it — dead bug or front plank — and do not keep re-testing it.

Worth saying once: abdominal exercises do not preferentially burn abdominal fat. Visible
definition is a function of overall body-fat level as much as abdominal development. The
core work in this plan builds the muscle. It does not decide whether you can see it.

### What I left out on purpose

Beyond the injury-related exclusions above: no jumping or plyometrics, because squash
already supplies impact and you do not need more of it competing for the same recovery.
No conditioning work, for the same reason — one squash session a week plus four lifting
sessions is enough load, and adding cardio to burn calories would be the first thing to
compromise recovery. No dedicated calf or forearm work, because it would cost session
time that pressing, rowing and abdominal work use better.

## Progression

**Double progression.** Each exercise has a rep range. Stay at the same weight and work up
through the range across sessions. When you hit the top of the range on every set, with at
least one rep still in reserve and no increase in symptoms, add the smallest increment and
drop back to the bottom of the range.

Fixed rep targets are the obvious alternative and they are the wrong choice here. The
reason is the increment problem: 2 kg on a pair is a 17% jump. With fixed reps, that jump
is your only way to progress, so you either take a step you are not ready for or you stall
for weeks. A rep range absorbs the progress between load jumps and turns one big step into
six small ones. It also means most sessions have a visible win in them, which matters more
for sticking with a plan than it sounds.

It also suits spinlock hardware. Plates stay where they are for weeks at a time; the reps
do the work in between. That falls out of the 2 kg increment and the screw collars; it is
not a preference you have expressed, and you have said nothing about how often you are
willing to restack plates.

**Add load when:** you hit the top of the rep range on all sets, with about 1–3 reps in
reserve, technique holding, and no meaningful symptom increase.

**Hold load when:** you have not topped out the range yet, technique is still settling, or
symptoms are stable but present. Holding is not failing. Most weeks are holding weeks.

**Reduce load when:** you cannot reach the bottom of the range with decent technique, or
symptoms increase meaningfully.

Effort target is roughly 1–3 reps in reserve. Week 1 is for finding out what the loads
actually feel like and should be easy — around 3 in reserve. Weeks 2 and 3 are where the
work happens. Week 4 consolidates and tells us what is ready to move up next block.
Training to failure is not required at any point.

**If you have been away for two weeks or more:** pick up at the next session in the
sequence, keep the same loads, and start again at the bottom of each rep range. Do not try
to resume where you left off. One easy week back is the price of not aggravating anything,
and it is a much lower price than another restart.

## How to use this

Sessions run A, B, C, D and then round again. Do the next one whenever the week allows.
There is no correct weekday for any of them.

With squash once a week, all four sessions should be manageable. If a second squash
session lands in a week, drop D — it is the lightest and most repetitive of the four. If
you can only get two sessions in, do A and B; between them they cover everything. Try not
to put A or C the day before a hard game, since both leave the legs tired.

Warm-ups are tick-off only. Do them properly, but you are not logging sets for them.

Log honestly, including the bad sessions. A log that only contains the good days is worse
than no log, because the next version of this plan gets written from it.
